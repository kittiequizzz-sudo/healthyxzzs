# Niche playbook: animated health and fitness science explainers

## What this niche is

These are faceless, animated YouTube explainers that turn exercise science, anatomy and health
research into fast, visual, myth-busting lessons. The viewer is a regular gym-goer, or someone who
cares about their health, and is frustrated by stalled results or conflicting advice. Each video
promises the mechanism ("what the science actually says"), not just tips. Trust comes from three
things: a consistent mascot character, simple diagrams of the mechanism, and explicit references to
studies.

Typical topics:
- Muscle-specific mistakes: lateral raises, curls, squats.
- Training variables: reps, sets, rest, frequency, range of motion, tempo.
- Physiology: hypertrophy, soreness, fat loss, protein, sleep, posture, joint pain, mobility.
- Myths and "what happens to your body when…" (fasting, walking 10k steps, quitting sugar).

## Anatomy of a video (from the references)

**Hook (first ~30 s).** Every beat is visual.
1. *Relatable pain:* "You do lateral raises every week, and your shoulders still look exactly the
   same." The visual is the character doing the exercise plus a week tracker, then a mirror shot.
2. *Curiosity gap:* "Here's what nobody tells you." A clean close-up of the character.
3. *Authority fact with proof:* "Your deltoid is the biggest muscle in your entire arm." The muscle
   wipes in red, BIGGEST appears, then a bar chart compares it with the triceps and biceps.
4. *Why it matters:* "…what makes you look wide." Outward arrows and a WIDE tag.
5. *The problem:* "Other muscles end up doing the job." Wrong muscles turn red, plus an X.
6. *The promise / list:* "Today, the five mistakes…" Five tiles pop in.
7. *The evidence promise:* "…what the science actually says about each one." Two scientists appear.
8. *Open loop:* "And the last one…" Tile 5 grows, turns orange and the others vanish.

**Each section, repeated N times:**
1. *Title card:* "MISTAKE 1" next to the character in the "wrong" pose.
2. *Show the wrong way:* the character does it wrong; the muscles actually doing the work turn red.
   Label the cue (HEAVY).
3. *Mechanism:* a diagram, a computer model or an anatomy insert. Use a lever-arm diagram for
   torque, a CRT skeleton for "researchers modeled", or a bone insert for impingement.
4. *Evidence:* name the study type in the VO ("in one study of lifters", "researchers modeled…")
   and put a matching tag on screen (1 STUDY, a group of purple people).
5. *The fix:* the correct form plus a green check plus a short label (THE TOP). Add a dashed guide
   line for the target position.
6. *Bridge:* the last line sets up the next mistake. The cut to the next title card comes right
   after it.

**Outro.** Recap the tiles (1–5, each with a one-word label), make a CTA tied to the open loop, and
end cleanly. No long end-screens inside the edit; the uploader adds those.

## Retention mechanics to keep

- A new visual every 2–3 s and a new overlay roughly every second. Dead frames kill retention.
- Graphics land a hair *before* the word, so the viewer feels the edit is in sync with their
  thoughts.
- Numbered structure with visible progress (the tiles), plus open loops ("the last one…").
- One idea per frame; the text on screen repeats the spoken keyword, never a different sentence.
- Pattern interrupts at section boundaries: a title card, a new prop, a change of scale.
- Use concrete numbers and comparisons ("more than double your biceps") and turn them into charts.

## Visual vocabulary (beat → default visual)

| VO beat | Default visual |
|---------|----------------|
| "every week / consistently / for months" | Week tracker with ticks, or a calendar flipping JAN→FEB |
| "still look the same / no progress" | Mirror shot (back view), callout circle on the muscle |
| "X is the biggest / strongest / most…" | Side close-up, red wipe on the muscle, title word |
| comparison of sizes / percentages / counts | Bar chart, highlighted bar orange, ghost box for "double" |
| "makes you look wide / bigger" | Arrows outward plus a tag |
| "other muscles take over" / compensation | Those muscles red, plus a red X |
| "N mistakes / N reasons" | Number tiles popping in |
| "science / researchers / studies" | Scientists with notepads; tiles above them |
| "researchers modeled / simulated" | Retro CRT with a wireframe skeleton, meter bar, curved arrows |
| "one study of N lifters" | Group of purple mannequins plus a "1 STUDY" tag |
| lever / torque / "heaviest when…" | Flat simplified figure with a double arrow showing the moment arm |
| joint / tendon / injury mechanism | Anatomical insert (bones) with an orange circle on the spot |
| "stop at / aim for" a position | Dashed orange guide line, green check, label |
| "heavy / light / drop the weight" | Dumbbells, HEAVY tag, down arrow, a smaller dumbbell |
| "most people do X" | Row of three purple mannequins doing it in sync |

## Health-claim rules (non-negotiable)

YouTube's medical-misinformation policy and the trust of this channel both depend on these.
1. Every factual claim maps to an entry in the source log. A claim without a source gets flagged
   in Phase 1.
2. Match the claim strength to the evidence. A single study is "one study found" or "linked to",
   not "proves". A simulation or model is "researchers modeled". An association is not a cause. An
   animal or in-vitro study must say so.
3. Numbers on screen must match the source exactly. If the script rounds them, say so in the flags.
4. No diagnosis, treatment or medication advice. For pain or injury topics, the VO or the
   description should point the viewer to a clinician. Suggest a line if the script lacks one.
5. Never invent study details (sample sizes, journals, years) for tags or charts. Draw only what the
   source log supports.
6. Unsafe exercise advice gets flagged even if it is sourced (for example, loading recommendations
   for beginners or anything for injured joints).
